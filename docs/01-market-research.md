| Fact | Value | Source |

**The structural fact the market is built on:** the official free supply is 160 static PDF questions with no engine behind them. Every player below exists to fill that gap — and almost all of them fill it badly.

> Critical UX fact: **the TTB exam booklet includes both the Turkish and English text of each question.** Candidates can read in whichever language they prefer.

## 4. User pain points (with quotes)

### Turkey-specific

1. **Translation quality actively causes harm.** ✅ Ekşi Sözlük:
   > *"türkçe sorularda ciddi şekilde tercüme hataları mevcuttur"* [there are seriously bad translation errors in the Turkish questions]

2. **Terminology collapses in Turkish.** ✅ Beyza Şanlıkün:
   > *"Error, bug, failure ingilizcede farklı anlamlara gelirken türkçede genellikle hepsi 'hata' başlığı altında ele alınıyor."* [While error, bug and failure carry different meanings in English, in Turkish they're usually all lumped together under "hata" (error).]

   This isn't cosmetic — the error/defect/failure distinction is directly tested on the exam. Gizem Evkuran makes the same point: *"Türkçesi garip gelebilir."* [The Turkish [terminology] can sound odd.]

3. **The problem isn't just content — the process itself is opaque.** ✅ This is why Sena Güner wrote her piece:
   > *"başvuru sürecini araştırırken çok fazla kör nokta olduğunu [gördüm]"* [I found there were far too many blind spots while researching the application process] — registration, online proctoring (camera on, screen sharing), how long results take… nothing documents this in Turkish.

4. **People study by trawling the internet.** ✅ Ekşi:
   > *"syllabus'u en az 3-4 kere iyi okuyarak çalışmanızı, daha sonra internet aleminde gezen soruları cozerek"* [study the syllabus thoroughly at least 3-4 times, then solve the questions floating around the internet]

   Sena Güner's actual stack: TryQA (34 sets) + Quizlet (600 questions) + FreeCram (240 questions) + Medium posts + Turkish Udemy. **Five sources, none of them consistent, all but one in English.**

5. **Reported effort and scores** ✅ Ekşi: 31/40 after 20 hours of study; 29/40 with just Udemy. Tactical advice: *"statement coverage ve decision coverage i iyi belleyin."* [memorize statement coverage and decision coverage well.]

6. **Ambivalence about the certificate itself suppresses willingness to pay** ✅ Ekşi: *"alınması kolay sertifika sorular çok kolay"* [an easy certificate to get, the questions are very easy], *"genelde bi esprisi olmayan sertifika"* [generally a certificate with no real point]. The counterweight: *"test mühendislerinin pasaporttan sonra, vizeden önce halletmeleri gerekir"* [test engineers need to get this sorted right after their passport, before their visa] and rising demand in job postings.

### International

7. **The tools aren't weak, they're wrong.** ✅ iOS review: *"buggy… yazım hataları, **sorular için kritik olan diyagramlar eksik**, bir kısım içerik ISTQB FL sınavından değil."* [buggy… typos, **diagrams critical to the questions are missing**, some content isn't even from the ISTQB FL exam.]

8. **Studying with dumps teaches the wrong thing.** ✅ istqb.guru:
   > *"memorised answers from v3.1 dumps are particularly dangerous because they drill the wrong answers into muscle memory."*

9. **Blanket distrust of the whole category.** ✅ testomat.io: *"Avoid internet tests as they might be inaccurate."* — the industry's standard advice is to not use this category at all.

10. **Where points get lost** ✅ istqb.guru + testomat.io: applying technique under time pressure in Chapter 4; Chapter 5 (%22 of the exam, chronically under-studied); *monitoring vs control*; *defect vs failure*; *verification vs validation*; *severity vs priority*; missing the words **BEST / EXCEPT / NOT**; 90 seconds per question and a lack of timed practice.

## 6. The opportunity space — 8 things that are consistently missing

1. **A Turkish-native question bank with correct terminology.** The entire free Turkish supply is the same 160 official questions; 2 of the 6 sets in the biggest paid product are retired v3.1. Nobody solves the *error/bug/failure → all lumped into "hata"* problem. **The highest-leverage item.**
2. **TR/EN side-by-side questions, like the real TTB booklet.** The exam already presents itself bilingually; the only product that mimics this is a Udemy course with 161 students. **A free, structural differentiator.**
3. **Learning objective (LO) and K-level tagging.** ~%60 of the exam is K2/K3, and everything in the syllabus is tied to an LO code like `FL-4.2.1`. **Not a single product I found has LO/K tagging.** Result: the question "which learning objective am I falling behind on?" can't be answered.
4. **Distractor-based rationale.** Most give the correct answer; the good ones explain why it's correct. **Almost none explain why each wrong option is wrong** — yet that discrimination skill is exactly what the exam measures.
5. **Visible, enforced syllabus-version integrity.** A *per-question version badge* + a public "retired content removed on date X" policy is a verifiable claim nobody else can make.
6. **Calibration to the real pass mark.** Guru99 passes at %50. The right product: real chapter weights, the 26/40 pass mark, 60 min, and a **readiness estimate** instead of a single percentage. (The 75-minute extension was considered and dropped — D-06.)
7. **Spaced repetition applied to exam questions.** Brainscape has the best SRS engine and zero vetted CTFL content. **Nobody groups a learner's own mistakes by root cause and re-serves them on a schedule.**
8. **Proper rendering of diagram-based questions.** Decision tables, state transition diagrams, control flow graphs are all examinable and break in cheap tools ("critical diagrams missing"). Responsive table/diagram rendering inside a question card is a real moat in the low-end segment.

**Runners-up:** per-question community discussion (ExamTopics' most loved feature, absent on the legitimate side entirely); an ad-free, signup-free, data-collection-free free tier; and Turkish process documentation (registration, online proctoring, retake rules).

## 7. UX references — patterns worth copying

**1. Duolingo — session shape and re-queuing mechanics**
Copy: fixed-length sessions ("a set of 10 questions", not an endless stream) → a study block has a visible end; **wrong answers get re-queued to the end of the same session** (the cheapest learning-reinforcement mechanic there is); a results screen that celebrates before it critiques; a forgiving streak mechanism ("streak freeze") → missing a day doesn't cause someone to quit.
**Don't copy:** the lives/hearts economy — paying to keep studying reads as hostile to a professional buyer.

**2. Anki (FSRS) — the repetition scheduler**
Copy: **four-button self-assessment** after the answer is revealed (Again / Hard / Good / Easy) — a lucky guess on a multiple-choice question needs to be scheduled differently from a confident answer; a **next-interval preview** on every button ("Good → 4d") → builds trust in the algorithm; a forecast chart that makes tomorrow's load legible; not letting two questions from the same LO come back to back (sibling burying).

**3. Quizlet — one piece of content, many modes**
Copy: the same 40 questions serving review, testing and warm-up alike (Flashcards → Learn → Test → Match); adaptive rounds that drop mastered items out of rotation; a one-tap "star" that builds a weak-item subset. And **card flipping as the core interaction** — question on the front, rationale on the back: that's already exactly what CTFL answer review is.

**4. LeetCode — the problem list and honest scoreboard**
Copy: a **filterable list with status / difficulty / acceptance-rate columns** → for us: chapter, K-level, LO code, your own accuracy, **global accuracy**. Showing global accuracy per question is a strong trust and calibration signal; nobody in this market does it. Also: per-item submission history, tag filtering, and **first-class dark mode** (the audience is developers; not one CTFL site I found advertises dark mode).

**5. AWS Skill Builder Official Practice Exam — the score report**
Copy: a **domain-level score breakdown** instead of a single percentage, with a bar against the pass line for each domain; a "recommended study areas" block generated from weak domains; a **question-by-question review pass** after submission — every item with a rationale + a citation to the official material. Directly applicable for us: every rationale citing a syllabus section (`v4.0.1 §4.2.3`) and an LO code. **The highest-trust feature that costs nothing beyond editorial discipline.**

**Bonus — Brilliant** for the question card: one concept per screen, generous whitespace, inline instant feedback instead of a modal, graduated hints (nudge → hint → full solution) — maps directly onto the K1/K2/K3 ladder.

## 8. Conclusion

**The international market is saturated on volume and bankrupt on trust.** The biggest free pool doesn't state its syllabus version; the cheapest paid pool markets itself as literal exam theft; a paid iOS app is still on the retired 2021 syllabus; the standard expert advice has become *"stay away from internet tests."* Meanwhile the world's best SRS platform has zero vetted CTFL content, and the best-featured app has **100 installs**. **Content isn't the bottleneck — trust and distribution are.**

**The Turkish market is structurally empty.** ~%2,9 of English demand by Udemy enrollment; fewer than 700 distinct Turkish questions in circulation (half of them the same official 160); no interactive Turkish product beyond a Microsoft Form; no discoverable Turkish YouTube/Telegram/Discord presence; a single accredited trainer on the list; and a 7.140 ₺ exam fee, a %25–30 failure rate, and nothing to buy between free and 30.000 ₺.

**The defensible wedge, at the intersection:** *Turkish-native · bilingual side by side · LO/K-level tagged · distractor-explained · version-badged · SRS-scheduled · correctly calibrated to 26/40.* Nobody does six of these in any language; nobody does the first two in Turkish at all.
